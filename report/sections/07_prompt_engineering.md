# 7. Prompt-engineering experiments

Requirement 11 asks for a reusable, parameterised template. `prompts.build_prompt(strategy, query, allowed_intents, **kwargs)` is the single entry point the pipeline calls, so E2 is the same pipeline run four times with a different `strategy`. All four strategies render the label catalogue, the tool catalogue and the output contract through the same three functions, and a test asserts that those blocks are identical, so a difference in results comes from the strategy and not from wording. The full prompts are in Appendix A.2.

- **Zero-shot** gives the instruction, the catalogues and the contract, with no examples. It is the floor for an instruction-tuned model and the cheapest in prompt tokens.
- **Few-shot** [13] adds four worked query-to-JSON examples, one per routing outcome, taken from the training split only (`data_prep.build_example_bank`) with every evaluation text excluded; the runner refuses to start if an example is also an evaluation query. We expected the examples to raise validity, by showing the format, and accuracy, by separating confusable intents.
- **Role and context** gives the model the persona of a tier-1 support agent on a live-chat channel, with the operating context: first-line triage, simulated systems, a colleague always available, and no authority to resolve the account. It targets reply quality and escalation, so it is read against the unsafe-reply and `requires_human` rates as well as accuracy.
- **Constraint-based** states a prohibition for each way output can fail: one JSON object and nothing around it, valid syntax, exactly the five model-owned fields, and a label copied verbatim from the catalogue. Its headline metric is first-attempt validity, since the aim is to rely on repair less.

All four ran on the 385-query sample under greedy decoding with at most 192 new tokens, through the unchanged pipeline (validation, up to two repairs, confidence gate, routing, safety screen). First attempts were generated in batches of 16 and repair prompts one at a time. Qwen's chat template adds a default system prompt to every request (§4.2); it is identical across the four, so the comparison stays fair, but the role persona sits in the user turn beneath it.

| Strategy | Accuracy | Macro-F1 | Valid, first attempt | Valid, after repair | Tool routing | Escalated | Unsafe replies | Mean attempts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| zero-shot | 40.3% | 0.362 | 95.1% | 95.3% | 62% | 28.1% | 17.4% | 1.10 |
| **few-shot** | **47.8%** | **0.450** | 83.4% | 94.8% | 68% | 21.0% | **3.9%** | 1.22 |
| role / context | 39.0% | 0.347 | 95.1% | 95.8% | 68% | 30.4% | 19.0% | 1.09 |
| constraint-based | 40.0% | 0.368 | 94.8% | 94.8% | 56% | 23.9% | 13.0% | 1.10 |

Accuracy counts a reply that never became valid as wrong. Tool routing is the share of the 50 rows whose intent should trigger a tool that were handled correctly, counting a correctly withheld `block_card` as correct (§11).

**Few-shot is the only strategy that separates from the rest.** The strategies answer the same 385 queries, so the comparison is paired. Few-shot was right on 55 queries where zero-shot was wrong and wrong on 26 where zero-shot was right (exact McNemar, p = 0.002); against role/context the split is 58 to 24 (p = 0.0002) and against constraint-based 59 to 29 (p = 0.002). The other three cannot be told apart (p from 0.47 to 1.0), so their order in the table is not a ranking. About 48% on a 77-way, fine-grained task is what a 1.5-billion-parameter model reaches with no training; the labels overlap by construction (§13).

**Two of the four design hypotheses did not hold.** Few-shot raised accuracy but lowered first-attempt validity, from 95.1% to 83.4%. All 53 of its first-attempt violations were a tool name outside the three-tool enum, such as `check_transfer_status` and `exchange_rate` (§10.1). The examples show tool calls, so the model plausibly learns to attach one to every intent; we did not test that. Repair recovered 43 of the 53, which is why final validity matches the others. The constraint-based prompt did not raise first-attempt validity (94.8% against 95.1%): all its failures were invented labels, which a rule against inventing labels does not prevent. The role persona did not make replies safer: 19.0% asked the customer for credentials, against 17.4% for zero-shot, though it handed over to a person more often (6.0% of replies set `requires_human`, against 2.6%).

**The safety result is the most useful one.** The screen withheld 65 of the 385 zero-shot replies for asking the customer for an account number, a card number or a secret. In all, 5.7% of zero-shot replies asked for a secret (a PIN, CVV, passcode or one-time code) and a further 11.2% asked only for an account or card number. Few-shot cut the total to 3.9% (2.3% asking for a secret); its worked examples contain replies that ask for nothing, and the model appears to copy that style. This is the failure §12.1 describes, now with a rate, and it is behaviour that accuracy, validity and routing all score as success. On the `card_arrival` / `card_not_working` confusion in §12.1, zero-shot labelled one of five `card_arrival` queries `card_not_working` and few-shot none; five queries are suggestive only.

Few-shot is the strategy used for E3 (§8): the highest accuracy and by far the lowest unsafe-reply rate, with its weaker first-attempt validity recovered by repair at 1.22 generations per query against 1.10.
